# Social Ace: evidence notes

Social Ace has not been tested in a trial. It is a rehearsal tool built from approaches that have research behind them. This file explains what each part is based on, how strong that evidence is, and what was changed after a review in October 2026.

## What the app is built on

| Part of the app | Approach | Strength of evidence | Key sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stories, "recipes" and "Say it out loud" | Rehearsal of social scripts: instruction, modelling, practice and feedback | Weak on its own. A Cochrane review found no clear effect of social skills training on social skills in children with ADHD. Rehearsal is used here as preparation for real-life practice, not instead of it | Storebø et al. 2019 (Cochrane) |
| Brave ladder | Graded exposure (a "fear ladder") from CBT for childhood anxiety: small steps, repeated until easier | Well established. Graded exposure is a core part of CBT for child anxiety, including parent-led versions used in the NHS | Creswell et al. 2017; Evans et al. 2018; Bayer et al. 2020 |
| Wobbly thoughts and the Brave thoughts game | Simple thought challenging from CBT: notice, check, choose a helpful thought | Moderate as part of CBT packages. The app uses "helpful" thoughts, not forced positive thinking | Evans et al. 2018 |
| Grown-up coaching (grown-ups page, missions) | Parent friendship coaching for children with ADHD-type peer problems | Promising. In a trial of 172 families, friendships improved in both the coaching group and a comparison group (gains lasted 8 months), with coaching slightly more helpful | Mikami et al. (Parental Friendship Coaching) |
| Praise guidance | Specific (labelled) praise; avoiding inflated praise | Labelled praise is a standard part of established parenting programmes; one well-known study on inflated praise | Brummelman et al. 2014 |
| Bullying stories | Assertive response, walking away, telling an adult, never hitting back; bystanders supporting the victim | Expert and school-guidance consensus; children themselves rate assertive and calm responses as better than hitting back; whole-school bystander programmes show small effects | Camodeca & Goossens 2005; KiVa (Blueprints); NI Direct and local-authority anti-bullying guidance |
| Quick games (faces, gap, arm's length) | Practice and engagement | Weak for transfer. Little evidence that skills learned in games carry over to real life by themselves | Storebø et al. 2019 |
| Stars and Finn's den | Token rewards for effort | Reasonable for motivation, especially with ADHD traits; kept calm, with no streaks or leaderboards | General behavioural evidence |

## Important limits

- **Social skills training alone doesn't do much for children with ADHD.** A Cochrane review of 45 trials found no clear effect on social skills or behaviour. This is why the app now points so strongly to real-life practice with a grown-up.
- **The app doesn't diagnose or treat anything.** If shyness or worry stops a child doing everyday things, or there are concerns about ADHD or dyslexia, speak to the school SENCO or a GP.

## What changed in the October 2026 review

1. **Standing tall.** Removed the claim that standing tall "helps you feel brave". "Power posing" effects have not held up well once selective reporting is taken into account. The story now says standing tall helps other people see and hear you, which is about how others read body language.
2. **Mistakes.** Removed "mistakes help our brains grow". Growth mindset interventions show near-zero effects in the better-quality studies (Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023). Replaced with "mistakes are how we learn".
3. **Wobbly thoughts.** Changed "wobbly thoughts are often not true" and "the wobbly thought was wrong" to checking thoughts "like a detective". This is closer to how CBT for children works: test the thought against what really happened rather than declare it false.
4. **Realistic endings.** Several stories ended perfectly. Some now include the wobbly feeling getting smaller over a few minutes (how exposure works), and that people don't always respond, but the brave thing was trying.
5. **Bullying.**
   - Removed lines suggesting that staying calm "makes the teasing not work" or "takes the fun out of it". This can make a child feel it's their job to stop bullying, or their fault if it doesn't stop.
   - Removed "that's what Ben wanted" as the response to getting upset. Feelings are now validated.
   - Added that stopping bullying is the grown-ups' job, and that a teacher will "help", not promise to "sort it out".
   - The bystander story no longer has the bully meekly returning the hat. The children walk away together and tell a teacher, which is safer than confronting.
   - Removed "That's your opinion" as a comeback, because it can escalate.
6. **Eye contact.** Changed to "a quick look at their face", and the grown-ups page now says not to force eye contact.
7. **Brave ladder.**
   - Reordered from easiest to hardest.
   - Each step is now done three times, because exposure works through repetition.
   - A "How did it feel?" check-in follows each try, with advice to stay on a step that felt very wobbly.
   - Grown-ups can add their own steps, because exposure ladders work best when they're personal.
8. **Grown-ups page.** Rewritten to put real-life coaching first, with guidance on praise, giving the child time to answer, bullying, and when to seek more help.
9. **Smaller fixes.** "Count to 20" became "count to 10" (more realistic for a 7-year-old with ADHD traits). "Watch their feet" became "watch their body". "Ask a grown-up their name" became "add one thing" (more natural with adults).
